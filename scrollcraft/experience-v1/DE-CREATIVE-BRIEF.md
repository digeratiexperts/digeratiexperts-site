# DE-CREATIVE-BRIEF — the DE experience (Phase 2)

Version 1, 2026-09-03. Written after the teardown, before the storyboard. Joe
should be able to read this and know what the site is trying to become.

## 0 · The audience leads (Joe, 2026-09-03)

DE works with small businesses: healthcare practices, law firms, accounting
and finance firms, real estate brokerages, nonprofits, professional services
firms, animal hospitals (the industries the site already serves). They come
for a reason: an insurance application or renewal with hard questions; a
message that almost worked, or an incident; a rule they now have to meet
(HIPAA, IRS/FTC, ABA expectations, wire-fraud exposure); tools and vendors
nobody owns; a managed-IT package to compare; downtime that cost them.

**Nothing we create may distract from who they are and why they are here.
That takes precedence over everything, including design; it leads design.**
Every act, line and object is checked against one question: does a practice
manager, a firm partner, a broker or an executive director recognise
themselves and their reason faster because of this? If not, it goes. The
real DE logo, as the site's header renders it, is the mark on every surface;
never a typed substitute.

## 1 · Thesis

**Your business is one system. We operate it that way.**

Digerati Experts is a cybersecurity-first technology operating partner. DE
protects, operates, modernizes and supports the whole business environment
(people, identity, computers and phones, email and communications, cloud
systems and business applications, network and site infrastructure, security,
continuity, recovery, support, advisory) as one connected system.

The visitor should **experience those things becoming connected**, not read a
list describing them.

## 2 · The reaction we are designing for

In order, as the visitor scrolls:

1. "That's a real business. I recognise it." (Act 1)
2. "I had no idea how much is going on under a small office." (Act 2)
3. "Oh. That's how it would actually happen to us." (Act 3)
4. "They didn't wait for the phone to ring." (Act 4)
5. "One system. They operate it as one system." (Act 5)
6. "There's a structured way in, and the first step is an inspection." (Acts 6–10)

And the summary reaction Joe named: *"I've never seen an IT company explain
what it does like this. These guys clearly operate differently."*

The site must feel advanced, intelligent, premium, calm, highly capable,
dimensional, controlled, original, modern, slightly futuristic, human. It must
not feel hacker-themed, cartoonish, sci-fi fantasy, generic SaaS,
gaming-oriented, stock-corporate, or like a network-diagram presentation.

## 3 · Design principles

1. **The visitor is inside a place.** The homepage's story layer is one
   environment the visitor enters, not a sequence of panels about an
   environment. Every act is a change of state of that same place.
2. **Transformation over replacement.** Objects introduced in Act 1 are the
   objects that connect in Act 2, get hit in Act 3, get contained in Act 4
   and resolve into one system in Act 5. No act constructs its own figure.
3. **Story first, proof beneath.** Two layers: the cinematic story layer
   carries emotion and memory; the evidence layer carries the operating model,
   service structure, assessment logic, proof and pricing clarity. The
   evidence supports the story and never replaces it, and it never leaks its
   vocabulary (hairlines, tiny labels, badges) into the story.
4. **Motion must teach.** Every major motion answers "what is the visitor
   learning or feeling because this moved?" Camera moves and light changes are
   the primary motion; sliding and fading blocks are not motion.
5. **Restraint.** Negative space, few words, one signature move per act.
   Copy under twelve words per beat in the story layer.
6. **Mobile is its own composition.** Every act defines what remains, what
   simplifies, what moves differently and what is removed at 390 px.
7. **Nothing generated without a job.** Every asset has an ID, a scene and a
   role in the asset bible before it exists. Built-in-code first; generated
   only where code cannot carry the material.
8. **Truth.** No invented numbers, telemetry, clients, timings or
   certifications, in the story layer or the evidence layer. The disruption
   in Act 3 is a plausible scene, labelled as an illustration in the quiet
   footnote, never as a client event.

## 4 · Visual principles

- **The world.** A small Arizona business at first light, built in code as a
  dimensional environment: an open floor with two rows of desks, six seats, a
  meeting table, a front door, a small network closet, a ceiling access point;
  screens softly lit; phones on desks; a few people present. Elevated
  three-quarter view. It is a *place*, drawn with light, depth and material,
  and it obeys `design/IMAGERY.md`: the idea of a working business, not a
  photograph of one. People are presence (soft, luminous, unportraited), never
  generated faces.
- **Light carries the story.** Warm low light for the living business. Cool
  graphite when the invisible system is exposed. A narrow amber for the
  disruption, never red alarm. **Magenta is DE**: it appears only where DE
  acts (a detection ring, a containment shell, a reset thread, a support beam),
  then the scene returns to clean white light when resolved. Violet stays
  lighting only, never a fill.
- **Materials.** Matte graphite surfaces, soft emissive screens, glass slabs
  for cloud systems above the floor, a translucent vault below it for backup,
  hair-thin luminous threads for identity and data paths. No neon, no glow
  bloom for its own sake, no wireframe globes.
- **Composition.** Depth from a real camera, not stacked parallax layers.
  Foreground, subject, background. Large areas of quiet ground. The type sits
  in the negative space, never over the subject.
- **Type.** Space Grotesk for the few display lines that matter; Inter for the
  evidence layer; Oxanium only for utility readouts. A new scale (Phase 6):
  hero display, narrative heading, section heading, body, evidence, technical
  label, metadata.
- **Evidence layer surfaces.** Paper and graphite plates, real product screens
  framed as proof, the diagram system where a mechanism needs explaining.
  Cards are allowed; a page of cards is not.

## 5 · Motion principles

- **Camera and light first.** Dolly in to a desk; rise to expose the layer
  above the floor; pull back to reveal one system. Light shifts state.
- **Persistence.** The same meshes and threads across Acts 1–5; state changes
  are interpolated, never swapped.
- **Tension and release.** Act 3 tightens (cooler light, tilt, an amber path
  creeping); Act 4 releases (magenta acts, the world levels, light warms).
  The heading correction from the Why DE passage is the reference move.
- **Scroll drives, nothing auto-plays.** Progress is bound to scroll. Idle
  micro-motion (a screen flicker, a breath in the threads) is allowed within a
  strict frame budget and pauses when the stage is off screen.
- **Pace.** The flagship sequence is about 25 seconds at a normal scroll, four
  to five viewport-heights of travel. Nothing lingers on an empty frame.
- **Reduced motion.** Five composed stills, one per beat, that carry the same
  meaning; the copy reads without any animation.
- **No scroll-jacking.** The page scrolls natively; pins release; the wheel
  is never captured.

## 6 · Narrative principles

- Each act has a beginning, middle and end; the page has one arc: living
  business → invisible system → disruption → response → one system → how to
  engage → proof → assessment.
- The business is the protagonist. DE is the operating layer: visible in what
  it does, not as a logo in the scene.
- Concrete over abstract: a message that looks like the bookkeeper's, one
  click, 8:12 on a Tuesday. Never "cyber threats are rising".
- Show, then say. The line lands after the visual has already made the
  point.
- The evidence beneath each act answers the question the act raised, in one
  screen, and offers one way forward.

## 7 · Rejected conventions

From Joe's lists and the teardown, all binding:

MSP marketing template · dark SaaS dashboard landing page · box-grid feature
list · architecture-diagram-heavy homepage · card-after-card · cyberpunk
wallpaper · hacker rain, hooded figures, padlocks, glowing circuit boards,
server rooms as mood · motion for motion's sake · sliding and fading blocks as
the motion language · generic AI-generated business people · stock filler ·
vendor-logo parade as trust · endless tiny labels · crowded node maps ·
generic glowing lines everywhere · "trusted by" styling · faux-enterprise
filler · compliance theatre · evidence badges shouting inside the story ·
template sections with different headlines · designing to the engine's
vocabulary instead of to a scene.

## 8 · Conversion goals

The visitor can always answer: who DE is, what DE does, why DE is different,
who DE is for, how to engage, what happens next.

- **Primary CTA:** Start Your Assessment.
- **Secondary:** Talk With DE · Handle Our IT (ProActive) · Solve a Business
  Need (solution families) · Get Support (DE Desk) · Client access
  (Marketplace, authenticated, kept separate from public storytelling).
- The assessment is the natural culmination: what gets inspected, what the
  business receives, why it matters, what happens next. Assess first, then the
  three doors.
- CTAs are sparse in the story layer (one persistent, restrained control),
  present at the close of every evidence block, and never a button row as a
  visual.

## 9 · The bar

The project succeeds when someone experiences the homepage and understands,
without reading a services list: DE sees the entire business environment;
understands how its systems depend on one another; protects and operates them
together; responds when something changes or breaks; keeps the business
moving. And thinks: *"I've never seen an IT company explain what it does like
this."*

The flagship sequence (Phase 5) sets the quality bar. If it does not make the
current site look small, flat and forgettable beside it, the direction is
wrong and we stop before expanding.
